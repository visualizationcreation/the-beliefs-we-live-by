# The Beliefs We Live By

Separate observations from assumptions, examine a prediction, and try a more flexible response. Eight readings connect belief, evidence and small experiments without promising that thought controls everything.

Book starting point: [Beyond Belief — Nir Eyal with Julie Li (2026)](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/761993/beyond-belief-by-nir-eyal-with-julie-li/).

Four connected paths through action, purpose, beliefs and relationships. These original guides use selected 2026 books as starting points alongside independent research. They are not book reproductions, author-endorsed editions or clinical assessments. Exercises are original invitations to reflect; no private answers are collected.

## Treat a belief as something you can examine

Nir Eyal and Julie Li’s Beyond Belief is a starting point for exploring how beliefs influence experience and action. This independently authored guide develops its own exercises alongside research. It is not a reproduction of the book, and it does not suggest that thoughts control every outcome.

Start by separating an event from its interpretation. “My proposal was not selected” records an outcome. “My ideas are always ignored” adds a broader conclusion. That conclusion may contain a real concern, but it needs more evidence than the single event provides.

Examining a belief is not the same as dismissing your experience. Unfair treatment and material obstacles can be real. The aim is to make the claim clear enough that you can decide what information, support or action would help. Sometimes the evidence supports a difficult conclusion; sometimes it reveals more than one plausible account.

### Try this — original exercise

Choose an ordinary recent event. Write what an observer could have seen or heard, then the meaning you gave it. Underline the parts of that meaning that would require more information.

## Make the prediction specific enough to investigate

A broad label often hides a more useful prediction. “I am bad at drawing” might mean “I cannot sketch a recognizable cup in ten minutes without a reference.” The second statement names a task, a condition and an outcome. It gives you something concrete to examine or practice.

This is an original reflection exercise, not a psychological assessment. Choose a low-stakes situation. You do not need to turn every worry into an experiment, and some questions require expertise or information that a personal trial cannot provide.

Make the prediction before you act so that you do not quietly change its meaning afterwards. Define what would count as partial success as well as failure. A cup with an uneven handle might still be recognizable. Better questions allow a more informative answer than an all-or-nothing identity label.

### Try this — original exercise

Complete: “In this situation, if I do this action, I predict this observable result.” Add a time frame and one outcome that would count as partial progress.

## Look for a plausible alternative, not a comforting story

In two experiments on social judgment, Charles Lord and colleagues found that considering an opposite possibility could reduce certain judgment biases. This is a bounded research finding, not a rule that every claim deserves equal weight or that the nicest explanation is correct.

Suppose a colleague replies to a long message with one brief sentence. Dismissal is one interpretation; being busy is another. A useful alternative should fit the available facts and suggest information that could distinguish the possibilities. You might ask a clear follow-up rather than decide the entire relationship from the reply.

Do not use alternative explanations to explain away repeated, documented harm. Evidence can accumulate. The practice is to keep an initial interpretation open long enough to examine it, while remaining willing to reach a firmer conclusion when the facts support one.

### Try this — original exercise

Write your first explanation and one plausible alternative. For each, name a fact that would support it and a fact that would weaken it. Identify what you do not yet know.

- [Considering the opposite: a corrective strategy for social judgment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6527215/) — Lord, Lepper & Preston · Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1984

## Test one small belief with a reversible action

A small experiment can make a prediction less abstract. Someone who expects every question to annoy an instructor might ask one relevant clarification in a workshop that welcomes questions. Beforehand, they can write the response they expect and decide what they will observe.

This exercise is a personal learning tool, not a clinical intervention or a controlled study. Keep the action low-risk and reversible. Do not test a serious safety concern by putting yourself in danger, and do not involve another person in a deceptive experiment.

Afterwards, record what happened before interpreting it. A short answer, an invitation to continue or no response are observations; “they thought I was foolish” is an inference unless it was actually said. One attempt will not settle a universal belief. It may still justify a smaller, more accurate prediction for the next similar situation.

### Try this — original exercise

Choose a safe, reversible action. Write the prediction, the observable result you will look for and the conditions that might affect it. Afterwards, revise the prediction only as far as the evidence allows.

- [Considering the opposite: a corrective strategy for social judgment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6527215/) — Lord, Lepper & Preston · Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1984
- [The interplay between goal intentions and implementation intentions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15574664/) — Sheeran, Webb & Gollwitzer · Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2005

## Growth needs conditions as well as a hopeful interpretation

A large United States school study found that a brief growth-mindset intervention produced modest academic benefits for lower-achieving students, with results shaped by context. It does not show that believing in improvement guarantees success, or that the same intervention works equally for every adult and every skill.

For a personal learning goal, pair a hopeful interpretation with practical conditions. What exactly are you practicing? Can you get useful feedback? Do you have time, appropriate tools and a task at a workable level of difficulty? These questions keep effort connected to a method.

Consider learning to explain a technical idea. Repeating a confusing presentation may not help much without feedback. Trying a shorter explanation with one listener and asking what remained unclear gives the next practice session a more specific direction. Improvement remains possible without becoming an obligation to succeed at everything.

### Try this — original exercise

Name one skill you want to develop. List a practice task, a source of feedback and a resource or condition you need. Change one of those conditions before simply increasing effort.

- [A national experiment reveals where a growth mindset improves achievement](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1466-y) — Yeager et al. · Nature, 2019

## Use language you can honestly stand behind

Experiments by Joanne Wood and colleagues found that repeating a positive self-statement could leave some participants with low self-esteem feeling worse. The result does not mean all encouragement is harmful. It does suggest caution about forcing a statement that feels sharply at odds with a person’s experience.

Try language tied to an available action rather than a sweeping identity claim. “I can ask for one example” may be more usable than “I am always confident.” “I have practiced this part twice” records something real. Neither sentence promises a particular result.

This is an original exercise in accuracy, not a requirement to talk to yourself in a prescribed way. You can acknowledge uncertainty and still choose a next step. The most useful sentence may be one that leaves room for both difficulty and agency: something is hard, and there is a specific form of support you can seek.

### Try this — original exercise

Take one encouraging sentence that feels untrue. Replace it with a fact you can verify and an action you can choose. Read the revision and remove any promise you cannot support.

- [Positive Self-Statements: Power for Some, Peril for Others](https://www.uni-muenster.de/imperia/md/content/psyifp/aeechterhoff/wintersemester2011-12/seminarthemenfelderdersozialpsychologie/04_wood_etal_selfstatements_psychscience2009.pdf) — Wood, Perunovic & Lee · Psychological Science, 2009

## Distinguish a belief from the barrier around it

Not every obstacle is an interpretation to revise. A course fee, inaccessible building, unpredictable shift or caregiving obligation can prevent participation regardless of motivation. Unfair treatment may require documentation, advocacy or institutional change rather than a more positive explanation.

Make a practical distinction among four things: the story you are telling, a skill you could develop, support you could seek and an external condition that must change. Several can be present at once. Someone may doubt their ability to return to study while also lacking affordable childcare.

Research on motivation and learning offers context for this distinction; it does not erase structural limits or prescribe this particular mapping exercise. The next action might be asking about financial support, arranging shared care, finding an accessible alternative or joining a collective effort. A useful belief is one that helps you see the situation more accurately, including what you cannot change alone.

### Try this — original exercise

Map one obstacle under interpretation, skill, support and external condition. Put each part where it belongs, allowing overlap. Choose the next action based on that map, not on a demand to think positively.

- [A national experiment reveals where a growth mindset improves achievement](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1466-y) — Yeager et al. · Nature, 2019
- [Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation, Social Development, and Well-Being](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf) — Ryan & Deci · American Psychologist, 2000

## Knowledge floor: what would change your mind?

An examined belief should be able to respond to evidence in more than one direction. Before a personal experiment, ask what would make you more confident in the belief and what would make you less confident. If no possible result could change it, you may be protecting an identity rather than investigating a prediction.

A single experience is limited evidence. Context, chance, other people’s choices and your own expectations can all affect the result. Repeated observations in comparable situations may be more informative, but they still do not create a controlled scientific study.

The unresolved question here is personal: what account best fits the information you have, and what remains uncertain? You do not have to end with optimism. A more specific concern, a revised expectation or a decision to ask for expert help can all be useful outcomes of clearer thinking.

### Try this — original exercise

Write your current prediction with a confidence level in ordinary words. Name one result that would strengthen it and one that would weaken it. Decide when you will review the evidence.
